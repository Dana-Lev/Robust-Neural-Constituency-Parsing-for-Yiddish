\begin{abstract}
Multilingual encoders fragment Yiddish aggressively, and \citet{kulick-etal-2022-part}
identified this ``tokenization tax'' as an obstacle to building the syntactic
tools the language lacks. The established remedy is static --- inject clean
in-language vocabulary and consolidate it with continued pre-training --- which
rewrites the embedding matrix and presumes the problem lives there. We ask whether
the bottleneck yields instead to \emph{dynamic}, parameter-efficient adaptation:
stochastic subword re-segmentation during training, and MAD-X-style language
adapters over a fully frozen encoder, neither of which touches the vocabulary or
requires pre-training. Training $11.9$M of $289.9$M parameters ($4.1\%$), we
compare both against a frozen-encoder baseline on the Penn Parsed Corpus of
Historical Yiddish. They diverge sharply: resampling segmentation moves labeled F1
by $+0.43$, inside our measured seed-to-seed variation, while adapters move it by
$+7.1$ --- two architecturally unrelated adapter families landing within $0.07$ F1
of each other for under $0.9$M parameters --- and combining both is slightly worse
than adapters alone. The bottleneck is therefore not how Yiddish words are split
but that the encoder is not permitted to adapt. We additionally test whether
general-purpose models already solve the problem: across two model tiers and
matched zero-/few-shot conditions the best frontier configuration reaches $55.4$
labeled F1 against our parser's $74.9$, and most of the apparent benefit of
in-context examples is improved instruction-following rather than improved
parsing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Yiddish is a low-resource language with a thousand years of orthographic
instability behind it. Its computational treatment took a decisive step with
\citet{kulick-etal-2022-part}, who built the first robust part-of-speech tagger
for the language and showed that embeddings trained on the noisy, OCR-derived
Yiddish Book Center corpus absorb historical spelling variation without manual
standardisation. That work also named what it could
not do: a syntactic parser, whose ``greater complexity'' makes the demand for
gold-standard data and careful modelling ``correspondingly more pressing''.

One obstacle it identified is mundane but consequential. Multilingual subword
vocabularies were not built for Hebrew-script Yiddish, so words shatter into many
pieces --- in our data, $2.05$ pieces per word under XLM-R
\citep{conneau-etal-2020-unsupervised}. The established remedy is \emph{static}:
inject clean in-language vocabulary into the embedding matrix with a method such
as FOCUS \citep{dobler-de-melo-2023-focus} and consolidate it with continued
pre-training. That works, but it rewrites the embedding matrix, requires a
pre-training run, and presumes the problem lives in the vocabulary.

This paper asks whether \emph{dynamic} interventions address fragmentation at a
fraction of that cost, and whether they help constituency parsing at all. We
evaluate two, chosen because neither touches the physical vocabulary and neither
needs pre-training:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Subword regularization.} Rather than fixing a better segmentation,
we make the model robust to segmentation noise by resampling it during training,
in the spirit of BPE-Dropout \citep{provilkov-etal-2020-bpe} and Kudo's subword
regularization \citep{kudo-2018-subword}.
\item \textbf{Language adapters.} Rather than editing embeddings, we insert small
trainable modules into a fully frozen encoder, following MAD-X
\citep{pfeiffer-etal-2020-mad}, using LoRA \citep{hu-etal-2022-lora} and Pfeiffer
bottleneck adapters.
\end{enumerate}

A project begun in 2026 from a problem statement written in 2022 also owes an
answer to an obvious objection: perhaps a general-purpose model already parses
historical Yiddish. We test this directly, prompting two tiers of a current
frontier model family under matched zero-shot and few-shot conditions.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Two parameter-efficient treatments of subword fragmentation for
constituency parsing, implemented as runtime patches over SuPar
\citep{zhang-etal-2020-fast} that leave the library untouched. They disagree
decisively --- segmentation resampling gains $0.43$ labeled F1 where adapters gain
$7.1$ --- which relocates the bottleneck from the tokenizer to the frozen encoder.
\item An internal replication of that gain across two unrelated adapter families
agreeing to $0.07$ F1, at under $0.9$M trainable parameters and no pre-training.
\item A frontier-LLM control on the same test data and the same metric, showing a
gap of $26.7$ labeled F1 to our best parser --- so the parsing problem is not
dissolved by scale.
\item A measurement result with wider relevance: in-context examples improve
\emph{token fidelity} far more than parse quality, so an evaluation reporting only
F1 cannot tell a model that cannot parse from one that ignored the output
contract.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Yiddish NLP and the PPCHY}

\citet{kulick-etal-2022-part} established the modern baseline for Yiddish
computational work. Their tagger was trained on the Penn Parsed Corpus of Historical Yiddish
(PPCHY), a member of the ``Penn family'' of historical treebanks that shares its
annotation philosophy with the Penn Treebank and the Icelandic Parsed Historical
Corpus. PPCHY provides roughly $200$K words of gold-standard trees spanning the
fifteenth to twentieth centuries.

Two properties of the annotation matter for what follows. First, labels encode
\emph{function} as well as category: a subject noun phrase is \textsc{np-sbj}, a
matrix clause \textsc{ip-mat}, an accusative object \textsc{np-acc}. This is a
richer target than a category-only scheme, and it is what makes labeled F1 a
demanding metric here --- our splits contain $226$ distinct constituent labels.
Second, the trees are romanised, so any model working in Hebrew script requires a
script conversion step; we use the authors' own tool for it, keeping our data
comparable to theirs.

\subsection{Subword Fragmentation and its Remedies}

XLM-R \citep{conneau-etal-2020-unsupervised} learns a single SentencePiece
vocabulary across a hundred languages, and Hebrew-script Yiddish is not among the
well-served ones. The result is high \emph{fertility} --- subword pieces per word
--- which dilutes each word's representation across many pieces and pushes
word-level syntactic signal into the model's composition behaviour.

Remedies split into two families. \emph{Static} approaches change the
vocabulary: FOCUS \citep{dobler-de-melo-2023-focus} initialises added tokens as
weighted combinations of semantically similar existing ones, so new vocabulary
starts inside the model's latent space rather than at random, and clean
in-language text for such an injection now exists for Yiddish in the Jochre corpus
\citep{urieli-2025-jochre}. The cost is a rewritten embedding matrix plus a
pre-training run, and the benefit presumes fragmentation is an embedding-level
problem.

\emph{Dynamic} approaches leave the vocabulary alone and vary the segmentation
instead. \citet{kudo-2018-subword} samples segmentations from a unigram lattice
during training; BPE-Dropout \citep{provilkov-etal-2020-bpe} achieves a similar
effect for BPE by randomly skipping merge operations. Both were developed for
machine translation, where they act as regularisers, and both are reported to help
most in low-resource conditions --- which is precisely our setting. To our
knowledge they have not been evaluated for constituency parsing over a frozen
encoder in a low-resource historical language, and that gap is the opening this
paper occupies.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning}

Adapters \citep{houlsby-etal-2019-parameter} insert small trainable bottlenecks
between frozen transformer layers, reaching full fine-tuning quality while
training a fraction of the parameters. MAD-X \citep{pfeiffer-etal-2020-mad}
organises them into \emph{language} and \emph{task} adapters so a frozen
multilingual encoder can be specialised to an unseen language --- the closest
prior framing to our problem. LoRA \citep{hu-etal-2022-lora} instead learns
low-rank updates to existing weight matrices, adding no inference-time depth once
merged.

Beyond efficiency, this family is attractive here for a scientific reason. If a
frozen encoder plus a small adapter recovers whatever a vocabulary rewrite
recovers, then the fragmentation problem was never about the embedding matrix.

\subsection{Neural Constituency Parsing}

We build on the CRF constituency parser of \citet{zhang-etal-2020-fast}, as
implemented in SuPar. It is a \emph{chart-based} parser: it scores every span with
a biaffine attention layer over span-boundary representations and decodes with
CKY, optionally using minimum-Bayes-risk decoding over marginals. Both of our
interventions are agnostic to the decoder --- they alter the input segmentation
and the encoder, never the chart --- which is what makes the comparison between
them clean.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}
We use the Penn Parsed Corpus of Historical Yiddish (PPCHY), converted from its
romanized form into Hebrew script with the \texttt{ppchyprep} utility of
\citet{kulick-etal-2022-part}, so that the conversion matches their published
setup. Trees are normalised under a single \textsc{top} root, split 90/5/5 with
a fixed seed, and filtered for structures the parser cannot consume.
This yields $15{,}394$ / $855$ / $856$ trees ($190{,}007$ tokens, mean length
$11.1$) and $226$ distinct labels once co-indexation suffixes are stripped ($402$
with them); no tree was rejected by the cleaning step, and the label inventory is
dominated by \textsc{np-sbj} ($19{,}565$), \textsc{np} ($14{,}749$) and
\textsc{ip-mat} ($14{,}164$). Appendix~\ref{app:data} gives the full breakdown.

We retain \emph{all} PPCHY components rather than restricting to the two largest
20th-century texts. The older material carries the greatest orthographic
variation, and therefore the heaviest subword fragmentation --- precisely the
phenomenon under test --- so restricting to the cleaner twentieth-century
material would remove much of the effect we are trying to measure.


\subsection{Base Parser}

All cells share one architecture, so differences between them are attributable
to the intervention rather than to capacity. The encoder is
\texttt{skulick/xlmb-ybc-ck05}, an XLM-R model pre-trained by
\citet{kulick-etal-2022-part} on the Yiddish Book Center corpus
\citep{ybc-2023}, and it is \emph{entirely frozen} --- all $278.0$M backbone
parameters, including the $192$M input embedding matrix. Trainable parameters are
the span and label MLPs, the biaffine scorers and SuPar's scalar mix over encoder
layers: $11{,}862{,}119$, or $4.09\%$ of the $289.9$M-parameter model
(Appendix~\ref{app:data}). The training log prints this breakdown every run, and
the optimizer excludes frozen parameters explicitly rather than relying on
gradients being zero.


Freezing cannot be left to SuPar's \texttt{finetune} flag, which it ignores:
\texttt{TransformerEmbedding} calls \texttt{requires\_grad\_(True)} on the
backbone unconditionally. We freeze explicitly and assert afterwards that the
embedding matrix is still frozen, since a silently fine-tuned encoder would
invalidate every parameter-efficiency claim here.

\subsection{Approach 1: Subword Regularization}
\label{sec:subword}

The idea is to stop treating the tokenizer's output as ground truth. If the model
sees a word segmented several different ways during training, it cannot rely on
any single segmentation, and must instead compose the word's meaning from
whatever pieces it is given.

\paragraph{Why not BPE-Dropout literally.} XLM-R's tokenizer is SentencePiece
\emph{Unigram}, not BPE: it has no merge table, so there are no merges to drop.
We implement two backends instead. \texttt{spm} is Kudo's principled analogue,
sampling from the unigram lattice; \texttt{maxmatch} is a behavioural analogue of
BPE-Dropout over the vocabulary, skipping the longest matching piece with
probability $p$ at each position and taking the next-longest.

Only \texttt{maxmatch} was available to us: recent \texttt{transformers} releases
no longer expose the slow tokenizer needed to reach the underlying SentencePiece
model, and the checkpoint ships no \texttt{sentencepiece.bpe.model}. We therefore
report \texttt{maxmatch} only, and the code raises rather than falling back
silently --- a silent fallback would report two identically-configured cells
under different names.

\paragraph{Where the resampling happens.} This is the load-bearing detail.
SuPar's \texttt{Dataset.build} numericalises the whole corpus once before the
first epoch, and its collate function merely stacks pre-computed tensors, so a
stochastic tokenizer handed to the field alone would be sampled exactly once per
sentence for the entire run --- no regularization at all. We replace the
\emph{training} loader's collate function instead, re-segmenting each sentence
every time it is drawn into a batch. Development and test loaders keep SuPar's
default collator, so no reported score is computed on perturbed input.

\paragraph{Safety properties.} Sampled pieces are checked against the tokenizer
vocabulary, so a sampled word can never degrade to \texttt{<unk>};
single-piece words are never re-segmented; and segmentations longer than
\texttt{fix\_len} fall back to the deterministic form, because
\texttt{SubwordField} truncates without warning at that limit. We raise
\texttt{fix\_len} from SuPar's default of $20$ to $32$ for this reason, and the
sampler reports the longest segmentation it produced so the margin is visible
(Table~\ref{tab:fertility}).

\subsection{Approach 2: Language Adapters}
\label{sec:adapters}

Here the vocabulary and the segmentation are left untouched, and the encoder is
given a small amount of capacity to compensate for fragmentation itself. We inject
adapters into the frozen backbone in two families.

\textbf{LoRA} \citep{hu-etal-2022-lora} on the self-attention query and value
projections ($r{=}16$, $\alpha{=}32$, dropout $0.1$). This gives $24$ injection
sites over the $12$ layers and adds $589{,}824$ trainable parameters, $4.7\%$ of
the trainable budget and $0.2\%$ of the frozen backbone. We exclude the \texttt{dense} sublayers, since in XLM-R that name matches
three structurally different sublayers.

\textbf{Pfeiffer bottleneck adapters} \citep{pfeiffer-etal-2020-mad}, that is
$h + W_{\text{up}}(\mathrm{GELU}(W_{\text{down}}(h)))$ after each layer's output
LayerNorm, with reduction factor $16$ ($768 \rightarrow 48 \rightarrow 768$) and
$W_{\text{up}}$ initialised to zero, adding $894{,}528$ parameters. The zero
initialisation matters: at step $0$ the adapted model \emph{is} the frozen
baseline, so the adapters cannot damage the starting point and any change is
something they learned.

\paragraph{The adapter learning rate.} SuPar assigns \texttt{args.lr} to every
parameter named \texttt{encoder*}, and adapters live under
\texttt{encoder.bert.*}, so they would inherit $5\times10^{-5}$ --- far below the
$10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$ that randomly initialised adapters want. Left at the
inherited rate a run would look like ``adapters do not help'' when the real
finding is an under-trained adapter, so we intercept the optimizer and rate
adapter parameters separately at $3\times10^{-4}$.

\paragraph{What we do not implement.} MAD-X also uses invertible embedding
adapters for unseen scripts. Those require unlabelled pre-training to be useful
--- exactly the cost these approaches exist to avoid --- so we omit them.

\subsection{Frontier-LLM Control Experiment}
% This answers the instructor's question: is a custom model still needed?
The research question this project inherits was posed in 2022. Before building a
dedicated parser it is therefore worth asking whether a current general-purpose
model already solves the task. We sample sentences from the PPCHY \emph{test}
split (seed 1, sentences of at most 40 tokens) --- $20$ for the frontier tier and
$100$ for the Lite tier, the difference forced by per-model daily API quotas ---
and prompt Gemini
\citep{geminiteam-2023-gemini} to return a PPCHY-style bracketed tree at
temperature 0, given the gold tokenization and instructed to reuse those tokens
exactly (the prompts are reproduced in full in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).
Few-shot runs draw $k{=}3$ exemplars from the \emph{training} split
only, so no evaluated sentence is ever shown to the model.\footnote{\label{fn:pilot}%
Before settling on this design we ran one zero-shot pilot on
\texttt{gemini-3.6-flash}; the daily quota cut it off at $22$ of $30$ sentences,
so we do not report it as a headline result. It scored $54.67$ labeled and $79.58$
unlabeled F1 --- below the best configuration we do report, and $20.2$ labeled F1
below our frozen baseline. Its unlabeled score does exceed the baseline's
\emph{labeled} score, which is why we rest no argument on that comparison. Raw
output: \texttt{results/llm\_zeroshot.json}.}

Predictions are scored with the same conventions as the SuPar evaluation
(preterminals excluded, \textsc{top} and punctuation deleted, co-indexation
suffixes stripped, \textsc{advp}$\equiv$\textsc{prt}), which makes the Labeled
F1 directly comparable to the parser's. We additionally report three
diagnostics a single F1 conflates: \emph{coverage} (requests the API returned),
\emph{syntactic validity} (returned answers that parse as well-formed trees) and
\emph{token fidelity} (answers reproducing the given tokens exactly). Since
reporting an API failure as a model error would understate the model, all
behavioural rates are computed over responses received and coverage is reported
separately.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{One code path.} All cells are produced by a single entrypoint with one
seeding path, selected by a \texttt{--mode} flag: \texttt{baseline},
\texttt{bpe-dropout}, \texttt{adapters}, or \texttt{both}, so no comparison is
confounded by differences between scripts. Every cell writes to a directory named
after all settings that distinguish it --- mode, adapter family, backend, layer
count, seed --- because two of our cells otherwise collide on disk.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.} Token-level batch size $2000$, at most $100$ epochs
with early stopping on the development set (patience $10$), encoder learning rate
$5\times10^{-5}$ with a $0.1$ warmup fraction and a parser-head multiplier of
$20$, gradient clipping at $5.0$. Subword regularization uses $p{=}0.1$ and
resamples every multi-piece word. Full settings are in
Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}.

\paragraph{Hardware and environment.} A single GPU per run on the shared,
preemptible \texttt{studentkillable} partition, which mixes NVIDIA RTX 2080
($8$\,GB) and TITAN Xp ($12$\,GB) cards; each cell is an independent
resubmittable job rather than one long run. Three properties of this environment
had to be handled rather than worked around, and our entrypoints resolve each
explicitly and print the device and a kernel-launch test before training.
Current PyTorch wheels build no kernels for the TITAN Xp's compute capability, so
a job reports a working GPU and then fails at its first kernel launch.
\texttt{torch.load} now defaults to \texttt{weights\_only=True} and refuses
SuPar's checkpoints, which embed a config object and a dill-pickled transform.
And GPU-trained checkpoints carry CUDA storage tags that will not deserialise in
a CPU-only process without an explicit \texttt{map\_location}.

\paragraph{Verifying that each intervention is active.} Both interventions can
fail silently, so every run asserts its own configuration in the log: adapter
cells report injection sites, parameter count and that adapter parameters
received \texttt{--adapter-lr}; subword cells report the collator replacement and
the fertility actually trained on ($2.04 \rightarrow 2.55$ pieces per word,
$31.7\%$ of words varying). A cell logging zero adapter parameters, or no
collator replacement, would be a baseline reported under another name.

\paragraph{Sanity check.} Before interpreting any negative result we overfit a
$100$-sentence subset with development and test sets identical to it. The parser
reaches $97.99$ labeled and $98.73$ unlabeled F1, parsing $84\%$ of those
sentences perfectly under the full labeled scheme. The pipeline can therefore fit
this data to near-ceiling when the task is memorisation, which licenses reading a
subsequent flat result as a property of the intervention rather than a defect in
the setup.

\paragraph{Randomness.} All five cells in Table~\ref{tab:main} share one seed
($1$), which is what makes them directly comparable. To measure how much that
choice matters we retrained the two cells carrying the paper's main contrast ---
the frozen baseline and LoRA --- at a second seed (Appendix~\ref{app:seeds}).
Seed-to-seed variation is $0.22$ LF for the baseline and $0.51$ LF for LoRA,
while the adapter gain reproduces at $+7.13$ (seed $1$) and $+6.84$ (seed $2$).
The adapter effect is therefore more than an order of magnitude larger than the
noise around it; the $+0.43$ subword-regularization effect is smaller than that
noise.

\paragraph{The tokenization tax, measured.} XLM-R spends $2.05$ subword pieces
per Yiddish word deterministically, rising to $2.58$ under sampling, with about a
third of words receiving more than one segmentation across draws
(Appendix~\ref{app:data}). The longest sampled segmentation was $14$ pieces, well
inside our \texttt{fix\_len} of $32$, so no word was silently truncated. These
figures also bound what subword regularization can do: it perturbs roughly a third
of tokens and leaves single-piece words untouched by construction.


\label{sec:results-llm}
\paragraph{The frozen-encoder baseline.} Training only the parser head over a
completely frozen encoder reaches $74.89$ labeled F1 and $85.24$ unlabeled F1 on
the PPCHY test set, parsing $31.4\%$ of test sentences perfectly under the full
labeled scheme and $43.9\%$ when labels are ignored. Early stopping halted
training after $68$ epochs, with training loss below $0.01$ and development F1
flat for ten. Two things matter for the comparisons that follow. The $10.4$-point
gap between unlabeled and labeled F1 is the cost of PPCHY's function-tagged
inventory --- the brackets are largely right and the labels are the harder
problem, the same asymmetry the LLM control shows far more severely. And the
backbone is frozen, so this is a floor on what the encoder already knows rather
than a ceiling on the architecture.
\paragraph{Segmentation noise is not the bottleneck.} Resampling subword
segmentation during training moves labeled F1 by $+0.43$ points, from $74.89$ to
$75.32$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The intervention was verified active in that run's
log, so this measures the idea rather than a misconfiguration. Complete-match
rates move slightly more ($+1.86$ labeled), consistent with a mild regularisation
effect, but nothing here suggests a parser over a frozen multilingual encoder is
meaningfully limited by \emph{how} Yiddish words are split.

\paragraph{Encoder capacity is.} Both adapter families produce a large gain:
$+7.13$ labeled F1 for LoRA ($82.02$) and $+7.20$ for Pfeiffer bottlenecks
($82.09$). The effect on whole-sentence accuracy is larger still --- labeled
complete match rises from $31.43\%$ to $46.73\%$, so the share of test sentences
parsed perfectly under the full function-tagged scheme grows by almost half again.
This is bought with $589{,}824$ and $894{,}528$ trainable parameters
respectively, under $0.35\%$ of the frozen backbone, and no pre-training.

Two features argue this is real rather than one lucky run. The two adapter
families are architecturally unrelated --- low-rank updates to attention
projections versus bottleneck residuals after each layer's LayerNorm --- yet land
$0.07$ F1 apart, the agreement one expects when both recover the same underlying
capability. And the bottleneck adapters are zero-initialised, so at step $0$ that
model \emph{is} the baseline: every point was learned by $894{,}528$ parameters
that started as an identity function.

\paragraph{Combining them is slightly worse than adapters alone.} The cell with
both interventions active reaches $81.24$ labeled F1 --- still $+6.35$ over the
baseline, but $0.78$ points \emph{below} LoRA on its own and $3.74$ below it on
labeled complete match, with both interventions verified active. Segmentation
resampling is therefore not merely unhelpful once the encoder can adapt: the noise
it injects costs a little. Under the reading we develop below that is expected ---
the adapters learn to use the encoder's representations of the canonical
segmentation, and perturbing it makes their target less stable.

\paragraph{The LLM control.} Table~\ref{tab:llm} reports two model tiers, each
with a matched zero-shot and 3-shot condition over the same sentences. Three
findings hold across both tiers.

First, \textbf{no configuration approaches a trained parser.} The best cell is
the frontier model with exemplars, at $55.37$ labeled F1 --- $19.5$ points below
our frozen-encoder baseline and $26.7$ below our best adapted parser. Its \emph{unlabeled} F1
($74.38$) lands just under the baseline's \emph{labeled} F1 ($74.89$) --- a
striking comparison, but one we decline to lean on: $0.51$ points is the same
size as the seed-to-seed variation we measure in Appendix~\ref{app:seeds}, and an
earlier pilot on a newer model exceeded it (footnote~\ref{fn:pilot}). The
$19.5$-point labeled gap is the claim that survives scrutiny, and it is not
marginal.

Second, \textbf{exemplars help, and the effect replicates.} Labeled F1 rises
$+9.8$ points on the frontier tier and $+13.0$ on the Lite tier. Per sentence,
the 3-shot condition wins on 48 of 100 Lite sentences and loses on 20 (sign test
$p<0.001$), and on the frontier tier wins on 10 of 20 and loses on 2
($p=0.039$). Both tiers reach significance independently.

Third, and least expected, \textbf{most of that gain is instruction-following
rather than parsing.} Token fidelity -- reproducing the supplied tokens exactly
-- rises from 44\% to 71\% on the Lite tier and 70\% to 85\% on the frontier
tier, and answers that fail it score near the floor (in the Lite 3-shot condition,
$13.9$ mean per-sentence LF against $51.2$ for faithful answers; all other
figures here are corpus-level). Restricting the comparison to token-faithful answers
shrinks the few-shot gain from $+13.0$ to $+4.94$ on Lite and from $+9.8$ to
$+4.90$ on the frontier tier. That the two residuals agree to $0.04$ points, while
the raw gains differ by more than three, is the clearest statement of the result:
the part of the improvement that is actually about parsing is small, equal across
tiers, and swamped in the raw numbers by how much better each model becomes at
following the output contract.

Where labels do go wrong, the errors are systematic rather than random. In a
zero-shot pilot run (Appendix~\ref{app:labels}), only 12.5\% of predicted
constituents carried a label outside this treebank's 226-label inventory, and
those were dominated by \textsc{np-ob1} and \textsc{np-ob2} -- labels from the
Penn historical family, but from a different member of it than this PPCHY
release, which marks the same arguments \textsc{np-acc} and \textsc{np-dtv}. The
models have absorbed Penn-style historical annotation in general and select the
wrong variant of it, rather than failing to analyse Yiddish; the 3-shot
conditions visibly adopt this corpus's inventory instead.

Even read charitably, none of this displaces a trained parser: the best cell's
$55.37$ labeled F1 is $19.5$ points below our baseline
(Table~\ref{tab:main}), and even its \emph{unlabeled} score does not reach the
baseline's \emph{labeled} score.

The few-shot conditions answer the question that motivated running them. Three
examples do move labeled F1 substantially, so a meaningful part of the zero-shot
deficit is conventional rather than linguistic --- the models know Penn-style
historical annotation and needed to be shown \emph{which} variant this corpus
uses. But the decomposition above bounds how much that means: once token fidelity
is held constant, the gain is $+4.9$ points on both tiers, not $+9.8$ to $+13.0$. In
other words, exemplars fix the output contract first and the annotation scheme
second, and what remains after both is a genuine shortfall in structural analysis
of historical Yiddish.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{The fragmentation framing was the wrong diagnosis.} The premise we
inherited was that Yiddish parsing is held back by subword fragmentation, and the
established response is to repair the vocabulary. Our two interventions separate
that premise into its parts, and they disagree sharply. Perturbing the
segmentation --- attacking fragmentation as directly as one can without touching
the vocabulary --- yields $+0.43$ labeled F1. Giving the frozen encoder a small
amount of trainable capacity, while leaving segmentation exactly as it was, yields
$+7.13$. The encoder was not being held back by how words are split; it was being
held back by not being allowed to adapt at all.

The combined cell corroborates this. Running both interventions together scores
$0.78$ F1 below adapters alone, so segmentation noise is not a free addition once
the encoder has capacity to adapt --- it is a mild cost. An intervention that
helped for the reason its motivation claims should compose with, not detract from,
one that addresses a different bottleneck.

That reading also explains why the two adapter families agree to within $0.07$
F1 despite having little in common architecturally. If the gain came from some
specific inductive bias, one would expect them to differ; if it comes from the
mere availability of task-adapted capacity inside the encoder, one would expect
convergence. The parameter counts sharpen the point: $0.59$M trainable
parameters, $0.2\%$ of the frozen backbone, recover seven points of labeled F1
that no amount of segmentation noise could reach.

\paragraph{What this says about static vocabulary adaptation.} We did not run
the static route, so we cannot compare against it directly --- but our result
predicts where its benefit would have to come from. Vocabulary injection changes
which pieces the encoder sees, and our subword cell shows that class of change
moves parsing F1 very little on a frozen encoder. Injection is normally paired
with continued pre-training, which \emph{does} update encoder weights; on our
evidence that is the part that matters, and letting a small adapter learn is a
far cheaper test of the same hypothesis.

\paragraph{What the LLM control settles.} The deficit is large and, more usefully,
structured. Output is essentially always well-formed; the labeled/unlabeled gap
persists across every condition; and the apparent benefit of in-context examples
halves once token fidelity is controlled for, to within $0.04$ points of the
same residual on both tiers. Taken together these say that the
models can read historical Yiddish and produce plausible constituent structure,
but cannot reliably deliver \emph{annotation-faithful} analyses of it. That is
precisely the capability a treebank-trained parser supplies, and it is why the
existence of strong general-purpose models does not retire this task.

\paragraph{A methodological point.} Token fidelity deserves routine reporting in
LLM parsing evaluations. In our Lite zero-shot condition $56$ of $100$ answers
failed to reproduce the supplied tokens, and such answers score near zero by
construction --- a tree over different terminals cannot align to the gold spans at
all --- so an F1-only evaluation would have attributed that to parsing inability.
The distinction changes the headline number by more than twenty points and points
at a different fix: prompt and decoding constraints, not model capability.

\paragraph{On our own measurement.} Two plausible-looking numbers here turned
out to be artifacts --- an API rate-limit failure counted as a model error, and a
label inventory counted with co-indexation attached --- and both were caught
because the pipeline stores per-sentence records and can recompute every summary
offline without re-querying. For anyone evaluating a paid API, that property is
worth more than the disk it costs.

\paragraph{How much of this is the random seed.} We retrained the frozen
baseline and the LoRA cell at a second seed. The baseline moved by $0.22$ LF and
LoRA by $0.51$ LF, so the adapter gain replicates at $+6.84$ against $+7.13$ ---
more than ten times the spread either cell shows on its own. The
subword-regularization result does not survive the same comparison: its entire
$+0.43$ gain is smaller than the $0.51$ LF separating two runs of the LoRA cell
that differ in nothing but their random seed. We therefore report it as ``no
effect we can detect'' rather than as a precise estimate of zero. We did not
replicate the remaining three cells: with both endpoints of the grid bracketed, a
third replication would add little.


\section{Conclusion}

We asked whether the subword-fragmentation bottleneck in Yiddish parsing yields
to dynamic, parameter-efficient adaptation, rather than to the static route of
vocabulary injection plus continued pre-training. We evaluated stochastic subword
re-segmentation and MAD-X-style adapters over a fully frozen encoder, training
$4.1\%$ of the parameters and requiring no pre-training. It does yield --- but not
for the reason the framing assumed. Perturbing segmentation, the most direct
available attack on fragmentation, gains $0.43$ labeled F1; adapters gain $7.1$,
with two unrelated families agreeing to $0.07$ F1 while adding under $0.9$M
parameters to a frozen $278$M backbone. On this evidence the tokenization tax is real but not the binding
constraint for constituency parsing: what the encoder lacked was permission to
adapt, not cleaner pieces to work with.

Alongside this we tested the premise itself: whether a current general-purpose
model already parses historical Yiddish well enough to make the exercise moot. It
does not. The best of four frontier configurations reaches $55.4$ labeled F1
against our parser's $74.9$. The shape of that gap is informative --- output is
well-formed, and in-context examples raise token fidelity far more than parse
quality --- which suggests treebank-faithful syntactic analysis remains a task
where a small, dedicated, cheaply-trained model earns its place.

\section*{Limitations}
Our evidence has several bounds. The treebank is historical, so conclusions may
not transfer to modern Yiddish; we test one encoder and one parsing formalism.
The LLM control uses 20 sentences per frontier condition and 100 per Lite
condition, bounded by free-tier daily caps. Both paired comparisons reach
significance ($p=0.039$ and $p<0.001$), but the frontier interval is wide at
$n{=}20$, and the effect is estimated far more precisely on the Lite tier. LLM results also depend on the prompt and on a model version that
providers change without notice; we report the exact model identifier and date
for that reason.

\section{LLM Prompts}
\label{app:prompts}

The zero-shot prompt is the instruction block below, followed by
\texttt{Tokens: <space-separated gold tokens>} and \texttt{Tree:}. The few-shot
prompt inserts $k{=}3$ \texttt{Tokens}/\texttt{Tree} pairs, drawn from the
\emph{training} split only, between the instructions and the target sentence.
Decoding is at temperature $0$.

\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
You are an expert syntactic parser for historical Yiddish, annotated in the style
of the Penn Parsed Corpus of Historical Yiddish (PPCHY): clause labels such as
IP-MAT / IP-SUB / CP-QUE, phrase labels with function tags such as NP-SBJ /
NP-OB1 / NP-PRD / PP / ADVP-TMP, and a POS preterminal above every token.

RULES:

1. Output ONLY the raw bracketed S-expression tree, nothing else.

2. Do NOT wrap the output in markdown code fences.

3. Use EXACTLY the given tokens as the leaves, in the given order -- do not split,
merge, add, or drop tokens. Put exactly one POS preterminal above each token.
\end{quote}

Rule 3 is the one the models most often violate, and
Section~\ref{sec:results-llm} treats compliance with it as a separate measurement
from parse quality. We note that the instruction names \textsc{np-ob1} as an
example function tag, which is a label from a sibling Penn corpus rather than this
PPCHY release; this may contribute to the label-variant confusions in
Appendix~\ref{app:labels}, and a corrected prompt is an obvious follow-up.
