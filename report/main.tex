\begin{abstract}
Multilingual encoders fragment Yiddish aggressively, and \citet{kulick-etal-2022-part}
identified this ``tokenization tax'' as an obstacle to building the syntactic
tools the language lacks. The established remedy is static --- inject clean
in-language vocabulary and consolidate it with continued pre-training --- which
rewrites the embedding matrix and presumes the problem lives there. We ask
whether the bottleneck yields instead to \emph{dynamic}, parameter-efficient
adaptation: stochastic subword re-segmentation during training, and MAD-X-style
language adapters over a fully frozen encoder, neither of which modifies the
vocabulary or requires pre-training. Training only
$11.9$M of $289.9$M parameters ($4.1\%$), we compare both against a frozen-encoder
baseline on the Penn Parsed Corpus of Historical Yiddish. The two diverge
sharply: resampling segmentation moves labeled F1 by $+0.43$ points, while
adapters move it by $+7.1$, and two architecturally unrelated adapter families
land within $0.07$ F1 of each other for under $0.9$M trainable parameters and no
pre-training. The bottleneck is therefore not how Yiddish words are split but that
the encoder is not permitted to adapt. We additionally test whether
the problem is already solved by general-purpose models: across two model tiers
and matched zero-/few-shot conditions, the best frontier configuration reaches
$54.2$ labeled F1 against our parser's $74.9$, and we show that most of the
apparent benefit of in-context examples is improved instruction-following rather
than improved parsing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Yiddish is a low-resource language with a thousand years of orthographic
instability behind it. Its computational treatment took a decisive step with
\citet{kulick-etal-2022-part}, who built the first robust part-of-speech tagger
for the language and showed that embeddings trained on the noisy, OCR-derived
Yiddish Book Center corpus implicitly absorb historical spelling variation,
removing the need for manual standardisation. That work also named what it could
not do: a syntactic parser, whose ``greater complexity'' makes the demand for
gold-standard data and careful modelling ``correspondingly more pressing''.

One obstacle it identified is mundane but consequential. Multilingual subword
vocabularies were not built for Hebrew-script Yiddish, so words shatter into many
pieces --- in our data, an average of $2.05$ pieces per word under XLM-R
\citep{conneau-etal-2020-unsupervised}. The established remedy is \emph{static}:
extract clean in-language vocabulary, inject it into the embedding matrix with a
method such as FOCUS \citep{dobler-de-melo-2023-focus}, and consolidate the new
tokens with continued pre-training. This works, but it is expensive --- it rewrites
the embedding matrix and requires a pre-training run --- and it presumes that the
fragmentation problem lives in the vocabulary.

This paper asks whether \emph{dynamic} interventions address fragmentation at a
fraction of that cost, and whether they help constituency parsing at all. We
evaluate two, chosen because neither touches the physical vocabulary and neither
needs pre-training:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Subword regularization.} Rather than fixing a better segmentation,
we make the model robust to segmentation noise by resampling it during training,
in the spirit of BPE-Dropout \citep{provilkov-etal-2020-bpe} and Kudo's subword
regularization \citep{kudo-2018-subword}.
\item \textbf{Language adapters.} Rather than editing embeddings, we insert small
trainable modules into a fully frozen encoder, following MAD-X
\citep{pfeiffer-etal-2020-mad}, using LoRA \citep{hu-etal-2022-lora} and Pfeiffer
bottleneck adapters.
\end{enumerate}

A project begun in 2026 from a problem statement written in 2022 also owes an
answer to an obvious objection: perhaps a general-purpose model already parses
historical Yiddish, and a dedicated parser is beside the point. We test this
directly rather than assuming either answer, prompting two tiers of a current
frontier model family under matched zero-shot and few-shot conditions.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Two parameter-efficient treatments of subword fragmentation for
constituency parsing, implemented as runtime patches over SuPar
\citep{zhang-etal-2020-fast} that leave the library untouched. They disagree
decisively --- segmentation resampling gains $0.43$ labeled F1 where adapters gain
$7.1$ --- which relocates the bottleneck from the tokenizer to the frozen encoder.
\item An internal replication of that gain across two unrelated adapter families
agreeing to $0.07$ F1, at under $0.9$M trainable parameters and no pre-training.
\item A frontier-LLM control on the same test data and the same metric, showing a
gap of $27.9$ labeled F1 to our best parser --- so the parsing problem is not
dissolved by scale.
\item A measurement result with wider relevance: on this task, in-context
examples improve \emph{token fidelity} far more than parse quality, and an
evaluation that reports only F1 cannot tell a model that cannot parse from one
that ignored the output contract.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Yiddish NLP and the PPCHY}

\citet{kulick-etal-2022-part} established the modern baseline for Yiddish
computational work. Their tagger was trained on the Penn Parsed Corpus of
Historical Yiddish (PPCHY), a member of the ``Penn family'' of historical
treebanks that shares its annotation philosophy with the Penn Treebank and the
Icelandic Parsed Historical Corpus. PPCHY provides roughly $200$K words of
gold-standard trees spanning the fifteenth to twentieth centuries, annotated with
part-of-speech tags and phrase structure.

Two properties of the annotation matter for what follows. First, labels encode
\emph{function} as well as category: a subject noun phrase is \textsc{np-sbj}, a
matrix clause \textsc{ip-mat}, an accusative object \textsc{np-acc}. This is a
richer target than a category-only scheme, and it is what makes labeled F1 a
demanding metric here --- our splits contain $226$ distinct constituent labels.
Second, the trees are romanised, so any model working in Hebrew script requires a
script conversion step; we use the authors' own tool for it, keeping our data
comparable to theirs.

\subsection{Subword Fragmentation and its Remedies}

XLM-R \citep{conneau-etal-2020-unsupervised} learns a single SentencePiece
vocabulary across a hundred languages, and Hebrew-script Yiddish is not among the
well-served ones. The result is high \emph{fertility} --- subword pieces per word
--- which dilutes each word's representation across many pieces and pushes
word-level syntactic signal into the model's composition behaviour.

Remedies split into two families. \emph{Static} approaches change the vocabulary:
FOCUS \citep{dobler-de-melo-2023-focus} initialises newly added tokens as
weighted combinations of semantically similar existing ones, so that new
vocabulary starts life inside the model's latent space rather than at random.
Clean in-language text for such an injection is now available for Yiddish through
the Jochre corpus \citep{urieli-2025-jochre}. The cost, however, is a rewritten
embedding matrix plus a pre-training run, and the benefit is predicated on
fragmentation being an embedding-level problem.

\emph{Dynamic} approaches leave the vocabulary alone and vary the segmentation
instead. \citet{kudo-2018-subword} samples segmentations from a unigram lattice
during training; BPE-Dropout \citep{provilkov-etal-2020-bpe} achieves a similar
effect for BPE by randomly skipping merge operations. Both were developed for
machine translation, where they act as regularisers, and both are reported to help
most in low-resource conditions --- which is precisely our setting. To our
knowledge they have not been evaluated for constituency parsing over a frozen
encoder in a low-resource historical language, and that gap is the opening this
paper occupies.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning}

Adapters \citep{houlsby-etal-2019-parameter} insert small trainable bottlenecks
between frozen transformer layers, reaching full fine-tuning quality while
training a small fraction of the parameters. MAD-X
\citep{pfeiffer-etal-2020-mad} organises them into \emph{language} and
\emph{task} adapters so that a frozen multilingual encoder can be specialised to
an unseen language, and it is the closest prior framing to our problem: adapting a
multilingual model to Yiddish without retraining it. LoRA
\citep{hu-etal-2022-lora} instead learns low-rank updates to existing weight
matrices, adding no inference-time depth once merged.

Beyond efficiency, this family is attractive here for a scientific reason. If a
frozen encoder plus a small adapter recovers whatever a vocabulary rewrite
recovers, then the fragmentation problem was never about the embedding matrix.

\subsection{Neural Constituency Parsing}

We build on the CRF constituency parser of \citet{zhang-etal-2020-fast}, as
implemented in SuPar. It is a \emph{chart-based} parser: it scores every span with
a biaffine attention layer over span-boundary representations, and decodes with
CKY, optionally using minimum-Bayes-risk decoding over marginals. There is no
transition system, action sequence, or stack. Both of our interventions are
agnostic to the decoder --- they alter the input segmentation and the encoder,
never the chart --- which is what makes the comparison between them clean.

attributable to the intervention rather than to capacity. The encoder is
\texttt{skulick/xlmb-ybc-ck05}, an XLM-R model already pre-trained by

Freezing is not left to the \texttt{finetune} configuration flag, which SuPar
ignores: \texttt{TransformerEmbedding} calls
\texttt{requires\_grad\_(True)} on the backbone unconditionally. We therefore
freeze explicitly and assert afterwards that the embedding matrix is still frozen,
because a silently fine-tuned encoder would invalidate every parameter-efficiency
claim in this paper.

\subsection{Approach 1: Subword Regularization}
\label{sec:subword}

The idea is to stop treating the tokenizer's output as ground truth. If the model
sees a word segmented several different ways during training, it cannot rely on
any single segmentation, and must instead compose the word's meaning from
whatever pieces it is given.

\paragraph{Why not BPE-Dropout literally.} XLM-R's tokenizer is SentencePiece
\emph{Unigram}, not BPE. It has no merge table, so there are no merges to drop and
BPE-Dropout does not apply as written. We therefore implement two backends. The
\texttt{spm} backend is Kudo's principled analogue, sampling from the unigram
lattice. The \texttt{maxmatch} backend is a behavioural analogue of BPE-Dropout
over the vocabulary: at each position, with probability $p$, skip the longest
matching piece and take the next-longest.

In our environment only \texttt{maxmatch} was available: recent
\texttt{transformers} releases no longer expose the slow tokenizer that grants
access to the underlying SentencePiece model, and the checkpoint ships no
\texttt{sentencepiece.bpe.model} file. We report \texttt{maxmatch} results only,
and note that the code raises rather than silently falling back --- an earlier
version of our own tooling did fall back, which would have produced two
identically-configured cells reported under different names.

\paragraph{Where the resampling happens.} This is the load-bearing
implementation detail. SuPar's \texttt{Dataset.build} numericalises the entire
corpus once, before the first epoch, and its collate function merely stacks
pre-computed tensors. A stochastic tokenizer handed to the field alone would
therefore be sampled exactly once per sentence for the whole run --- no
regularization at all. We instead replace the \emph{training} loader's collate
function so that each sentence is re-segmented every time it is drawn into a
batch. Development and test loaders keep SuPar's default collator and stay
deterministic, so no reported score is ever computed on perturbed input.

\paragraph{Safety properties.} Sampled pieces are checked against the tokenizer
vocabulary, so a sampled word can never degrade to \texttt{<unk>};
single-piece words are never re-segmented; and segmentations longer than
\texttt{fix\_len} fall back to the deterministic form, because
\texttt{SubwordField} truncates without warning at that limit. We raise
\texttt{fix\_len} from SuPar's default of $20$ to $32$ for this reason, and the
sampler reports the longest segmentation it produced so the margin is visible
(Table~\ref{tab:fertility}).

\subsection{Approach 2: Language Adapters}
\label{sec:adapters}

Here the vocabulary and the segmentation are left untouched, and the encoder is
given a small amount of capacity to compensate for fragmentation itself. We inject
adapters into the frozen backbone in two families.

\textbf{LoRA} \citep{hu-etal-2022-lora} on the self-attention query and value
projections ($r{=}16$, $\alpha{=}32$, dropout $0.1$). This gives $24$ injection
sites over the $12$ layers and adds $589{,}824$ trainable parameters, $4.7\%$ of
the trainable budget and $0.2\%$ of the frozen backbone. We deliberately exclude the \texttt{dense} sublayers from
the target list: in XLM-R that name matches three structurally different
sublayers, making the configuration ambiguous.

\textbf{Pfeiffer bottleneck adapters} \citep{pfeiffer-etal-2020-mad}, that is
$h + W_{\text{up}}(\mathrm{GELU}(W_{\text{down}}(h)))$ after each layer's output
LayerNorm, with reduction factor $16$ ($768 \rightarrow 48 \rightarrow 768$) and
$W_{\text{up}}$ initialised to zero, adding $894{,}528$ parameters. The zero
initialisation matters: at step $0$ the adapted model \emph{is} the frozen
baseline, so the adapters cannot damage the starting point and any change is
something they learned.

\paragraph{The adapter learning rate.} SuPar assigns \texttt{args.lr} to every
parameter whose name begins with \texttt{encoder}, and adapters live under
\texttt{encoder.bert.*}. They would therefore inherit $5\times10^{-5}$, far too
low for randomly initialised adapter weights, which typically want
$10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$. A run left at the inherited rate would look like
``adapters do not help'' when the real finding is an under-trained adapter, so we
intercept the optimizer and rate adapter parameters separately, sweeping
$\{1,3,10\}\times10^{-4}$.

\paragraph{What we do not implement.} MAD-X also uses invertible adapters on the
embedding layer for unseen scripts. Those are the component that requires
unlabelled pre-training to be useful, which is exactly the cost these approaches
exist to avoid, so we omit them and note the omission.

\subsection{Frontier-LLM Control Experiment}
% This answers the instructor's question: is a custom model still needed?
The research question this project inherits was posed in 2022. Before building a
dedicated parser it is therefore worth asking whether a current general-purpose
model already solves the task. We sample 30 sentences from the PPCHY
\emph{test} split (seed 1, sentences of at most 40 tokens) and prompt Gemini
\citep{geminiteam-2023-gemini} to return a PPCHY-style bracketed tree at
temperature 0, given the gold tokenization and instructed to reuse those tokens
exactly. Few-shot runs draw $k{=}3$ exemplars from the \emph{training} split
only, so no evaluated sentence is ever shown to the model.

Predictions are scored with the same conventions as the SuPar evaluation
(preterminals excluded, \textsc{top} and punctuation deleted, co-indexation
suffixes stripped, \textsc{advp}$\equiv$\textsc{prt}), which makes the Labeled
F1 directly comparable to the parser's. We additionally report three diagnostics
that a single F1 conflates: \emph{coverage}, the share of requests the API
actually returned; \emph{syntactic validity}, the share of returned answers that
parse as well-formed trees; and \emph{token fidelity}, the share that reproduce
the given tokens exactly. Reporting an API failure as a model error would
understate the model, so all behavioural rates are computed over responses
received and coverage is reported separately.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{One code path.} All cells are produced by a single entrypoint with one
seeding path, selected by a \texttt{--mode} flag: \texttt{baseline},
\texttt{bpe-dropout}, \texttt{adapters}, or \texttt{both}. \texttt{baseline}
reproduces the frozen-encoder configuration exactly, so the comparison is not
confounded by differences between scripts. Every cell writes to a directory named
after all settings that distinguish it --- mode, adapter family, backend, layer
count, seed --- because two of our cells otherwise collide on disk.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.} Token-level batch size $2000$, at most $100$ epochs
with early stopping on the development set (patience $10$), encoder learning rate
$5\times10^{-5}$ with a $0.1$ warmup fraction and a parser-head multiplier of
$20$, gradient clipping at $5.0$. Subword regularization uses $p{=}0.1$ and
resamples every multi-piece word. Full settings are in
Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}.

\paragraph{Hardware.} A single GPU per run on the university's shared
\texttt{studentkillable} partition, which mixes NVIDIA RTX 2080 ($8$\,GB) and
TITAN Xp ($12$\,GB) cards. Two properties of that environment shaped our setup.
The partition is preemptible, so each cell is an independent job that can be
resubmitted rather than one long run; and current PyTorch wheels build no kernels
for the TITAN Xp's compute capability, which caused a job to report a working GPU
and then fail at its first kernel launch --- so our training entrypoint now
launches a probe kernel and refuses to start if it fails.

\paragraph{Verifying that each intervention is active.} Both interventions can
fail silently, so each run asserts its own configuration in the log. The adapter
cells report the injection sites and parameter count and confirm that adapter
parameters received \texttt{--adapter-lr} rather than the encoder rate; the
subword-regularization cells report that the training loader was replaced and
print the fertility they are actually training with ($2.04 \rightarrow 2.55$
pieces per word, $31.7\%$ of words varying). A cell whose log shows zero adapter
parameters, or no collator replacement, would be a baseline reported under
another name.

\paragraph{Reproducibility notes.} SuPar 1.1.4 predates the current PyTorch
line, and three incompatibilities had to be handled rather than worked around.
\texttt{torch.load} now defaults to \texttt{weights\_only=True} and refuses
SuPar's checkpoints, which embed a config object and a dill-pickled transform;
checkpoints trained on a GPU additionally carry CUDA storage tags that will not
deserialise in a CPU-only process without an explicit \texttt{map\_location}; and
current wheels build no kernels for the compute capability of some cards in our
shared partition, which produces a job that reports a working GPU and then fails
at its first kernel launch. Our entrypoints resolve all three explicitly, and
print the device and its kernel-launch test before training, so a
misconfiguration is visible in the first seconds of a log rather than an hour in.

\paragraph{Sanity check.} Following the course guidance to demonstrate that
training works before interpreting any negative result, we first overfit a
$100$-sentence subset with development and test sets identical to it. The parser
reaches $97.99$ labeled F1 and $98.73$ unlabeled F1 there, parsing $84\%$ of
those sentences perfectly under the full labeled scheme. The pipeline can
therefore fit this data to near-ceiling when the task is memorisation, which is
what licenses reading any subsequent flat result as a property of the
intervention rather than a defect in the setup.

\paragraph{Randomness.} Every cell reported here is a single seed ($1$), stated
plainly rather than implying more. The seven-point adapter gap is far too large to
be seed noise at this corpus size, and the independent agreement of two adapter
families provides a form of replication, but the $+0.43$ subword-regularization
result sits inside the range a seed sweep could move.

\label{sec:results-llm}
\paragraph{The frozen-encoder baseline.} Training only the parser head over a
completely frozen encoder reaches $74.89$ labeled F1 and $85.24$ unlabeled F1 on
the PPCHY test set, parsing $31.4\%$ of test sentences perfectly under the full
labeled scheme and $43.9\%$ when labels are ignored. Early stopping halted
training after $68$ epochs, at which point training loss had fallen below $0.01$
while development F1 had been flat for ten epochs --- the model had extracted what
it could from the data it was given. Two things are worth noting for the
comparisons that follow. The $10.4$-point gap between unlabeled and labeled F1 is
the cost of PPCHY's function-tagged inventory: the brackets are largely right and
the labels are the harder problem, which is the same asymmetry the LLM control
exhibits far more severely. And this number is obtained with a frozen backbone, so
it is a floor for what the encoder already knows rather than a ceiling on the
architecture.
\paragraph{Segmentation noise is not the bottleneck.} Resampling subword
segmentation during training moves labeled F1 by $+0.43$ points, from $74.89$ to
$75.32$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The intervention was demonstrably active --- the
training loader resampled every batch, fertility rose from $2.04$ to $2.55$
pieces per word, and $31.7\%$ of words received more than one segmentation --- so
this is a measurement of the idea, not of a misconfiguration. Complete-match
rates move slightly more ($+1.86$ labeled), consistent with a mild regularisation
effect, but nothing here suggests that a parser over a frozen multilingual
encoder is meaningfully limited by \emph{how} Yiddish words are split.

\paragraph{Encoder capacity is.} Both adapter families produce a large gain:
$+7.13$ labeled F1 for LoRA ($82.02$) and $+7.20$ for Pfeiffer bottlenecks
($82.09$). The effect on whole-sentence accuracy is larger still --- labeled
complete match rises from $31.43\%$ to $46.73\%$, so the share of test sentences
parsed perfectly under the full function-tagged scheme grows by almost half again.
This is bought with $589{,}824$ and $894{,}528$ trainable parameters
respectively, under $0.35\%$ of the frozen backbone, and no pre-training.

Two features of this result argue that it is real rather than an artifact of one
lucky run. The two adapter families are architecturally unrelated --- low-rank
updates to attention projections versus bottleneck residuals after each layer's
LayerNorm --- yet they land $0.07$ F1 apart, which is the kind of agreement one
expects when both are recovering the same underlying capability. And the bottleneck
adapters are zero-initialised, so at step $0$ that model \emph{is} the baseline;
every point of the gain was learned by $894{,}528$ parameters that started as an
identity function.

\paragraph{The LLM control.} Table~\ref{tab:llm} reports two model tiers, each
with a matched zero-shot and 3-shot condition over the same sentences. Three
findings hold across both tiers.

First, \textbf{no configuration approaches a trained parser.} The best cell is
the frontier model with exemplars, at $54.24$ labeled F1 --- $20.7$ points below
our frozen-encoder baseline and $27.9$ below our best adapted parser. Its \emph{unlabeled} F1
($73.73$) does not even reach the baseline's \emph{labeled} F1 ($74.89$): the
parser assigns full function-tagged labels more accurately than the language
model draws unlabeled brackets. A dedicated parser is therefore not made
redundant by current general-purpose models, and the margin is not marginal.

Second, \textbf{exemplars help, and the effect replicates.} Labeled F1 rises
$+8.7$ points on the frontier tier and $+13.0$ on the Lite tier. Per sentence,
the 3-shot condition is better on 48 of 100 Lite sentences and worse on 20
(sign test $p<0.001$); on the smaller frontier sample it is better on 9 of 19
and worse on 2 ($p=0.07$).

Third, and least expected, \textbf{most of that gain is instruction-following
rather than parsing.} Token fidelity -- reproducing the supplied tokens exactly
-- rises from 44\% to 71\% on the Lite tier and 70\% to 84\% on the frontier
tier, and answers that fail it score near the floor (13.9 LF on Lite, versus
51.2 for faithful answers). Restricting the comparison to token-faithful
answers shrinks the few-shot gain from $+13.0$ to $+4.9$ points on Lite and
from $+8.7$ to $+3.7$ on the frontier tier. What the exemplars mainly teach is
the output contract, not Yiddish syntax.

Where labels do go wrong, the errors are systematic rather than random. In a
zero-shot pilot run (Appendix~\ref{app:labels}), only 12.5\% of predicted
constituents carried a label outside this treebank's 226-label inventory, and
those were dominated by \textsc{np-ob1} and \textsc{np-ob2} -- labels from the
Penn historical family, but from a different member of it than this PPCHY
release, which marks the same arguments \textsc{np-acc} and \textsc{np-dtv}. The
models have absorbed Penn-style historical annotation in general and select the
wrong variant of it, rather than failing to analyse Yiddish; the 3-shot
conditions visibly adopt this corpus's inventory instead.

Even read charitably, none of this displaces a trained parser: the best cell's
$54.24$ labeled F1 is $20.7$ points below our baseline
(Table~\ref{tab:main}), and even its \emph{unlabeled} score does not reach the
baseline's \emph{labeled} score.

The few-shot conditions answer the question that motivated running them. Three
examples do move labeled F1 substantially, so a meaningful part of the zero-shot
deficit is conventional rather than linguistic --- the models know Penn-style
historical annotation and needed to be shown \emph{which} variant this corpus
uses. But the decomposition above bounds how much that means: once token fidelity
is held constant, the gain is $+3.7$ to $+4.9$ points, not $+8.7$ to $+13.0$. In
other words, exemplars fix the output contract first and the annotation scheme
second, and what remains after both is a genuine shortfall in structural analysis
of historical Yiddish.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{The fragmentation framing was the wrong diagnosis.} The premise we
inherited was that Yiddish parsing is held back by subword fragmentation, and the
established response is to repair the vocabulary. Our two interventions separate
that premise into its parts, and they disagree sharply. Perturbing the
segmentation --- attacking fragmentation as directly as one can without touching
the vocabulary --- yields $+0.43$ labeled F1. Giving the frozen encoder a small
amount of trainable capacity, while leaving segmentation exactly as it was, yields
$+7.13$. The encoder was not being held back by how words are split; it was being
held back by not being allowed to adapt at all.

That reading also explains why the two adapter families agree to within $0.07$
F1 despite having little in common architecturally. If the gain came from some
specific inductive bias, one would expect them to differ; if it comes from the
mere availability of task-adapted capacity inside the encoder, one would expect
convergence. The parameter counts sharpen the point: $0.59$M trainable
parameters, $0.2\%$ of the frozen backbone, recover seven points of labeled F1
that no amount of segmentation noise could reach.

\paragraph{What this says about static vocabulary adaptation.} We did not run the
static route, so we cannot compare directly against it. But our result predicts
where its benefit would have to come from. Vocabulary injection changes which
pieces the encoder sees; our subword-regularization cell shows that this class of
change moves parsing F1 very little on a frozen encoder. Injection is also
normally paired with continued pre-training, which \emph{does} update encoder
weights --- and that, on our evidence, is the part that would matter. A cheaper
test of the same hypothesis is simply to let a small adapter learn.

\paragraph{What the LLM control settles.} The deficit is large and, more usefully,
structured. Output is essentially always well-formed; the labeled/unlabeled gap
persists across every condition; and the apparent benefit of in-context examples
halves once token fidelity is controlled for. Taken together these say that the
models can read historical Yiddish and produce plausible constituent structure,
but cannot reliably deliver \emph{annotation-faithful} analyses of it. That is
precisely the capability a treebank-trained parser supplies, and it is why the
existence of strong general-purpose models does not retire this task.

\paragraph{A methodological point.} Token fidelity deserves to be reported
routinely in LLM parsing evaluations. In our Lite zero-shot condition, $56$ of
$100$ answers failed to reproduce the supplied tokens, and those answers score
near zero by construction --- a tree over different terminals cannot align to the
gold spans at all. An evaluation reporting only F1 would have attributed that to
parsing inability. The distinction is not cosmetic: it changes the headline number
by more than twenty points and it points at a different fix (prompt and decoding
constraints, rather than model capability).

\paragraph{On our own measurement.} We twice found that a plausible-looking
number was an artifact rather than a result: an API rate-limit failure counted as
a model error, and a label inventory counted with co-indexation attached. Both
were caught because the pipeline stores per-sentence records and can recompute
summaries without re-querying. We would recommend that design to anyone
evaluating a paid API: the ability to re-derive every metric offline is worth more
than the disk it costs.

\paragraph{A caveat we cannot remove.} Every cell here is a single seed. The
$+7$ point gap is far too large to be seed noise at this corpus size, and the
independent agreement of two adapter families is reassuring, but the $+0.43$
subword-regularization result is well within the range where a seed sweep could
change its sign. We report it as ``no meaningful effect'', not as a precise
estimate of zero.


\section{Conclusion}

We asked whether the subword-fragmentation bottleneck in Yiddish parsing yields
to dynamic, parameter-efficient adaptation, rather than to the static route of
vocabulary injection plus continued pre-training. We evaluated stochastic subword
re-segmentation and MAD-X-style
adapters over a fully frozen encoder, training $4.1\%$ of the model's parameters
and requiring no pre-training at all. It does yield --- but not for the reason the
framing assumed. Perturbing segmentation, the most direct available attack on
fragmentation, gains $0.43$ labeled F1; adapters gain $7.1$, with two unrelated
families agreeing to $0.07$ F1 while adding under $0.9$M parameters to a frozen
$278$M backbone. On this evidence the tokenization tax is real but not the binding
constraint for constituency parsing: what the encoder lacked was permission to
adapt, not cleaner pieces to work with.

Alongside this we tested the premise itself: whether a current general-purpose
model already parses historical Yiddish well enough to make the exercise moot. It
does not. The best of four frontier configurations reaches $54.2$ labeled F1
against our parser's $74.9$, and its unlabeled score does not reach the parser's
labeled score. The shape of that gap is informative --- output
is well-formed, and in-context examples raise token fidelity far more than parse
quality --- which suggests that treebank-faithful syntactic analysis remains a
task where a small, dedicated, cheaply-trained model earns its place.

\section*{Limitations}
Our evidence has several bounds. The treebank is historical, so conclusions may
not transfer to modern Yiddish; we test one encoder and one parsing formalism.
The LLM control uses 20 sentences per frontier condition and 100 per Lite
condition; free-tier daily caps bounded the former, so the frontier comparison
in particular has a wide confidence interval and the sign test over 19 paired
sentences only reaches $p=0.07$. The Lite tier, where 100 sentences were
affordable, is where the few-shot effect is statistically clear. LLM results also depend on the prompt and on a model version that

\section{LLM Prompts}
\label{app:prompts}

The zero-shot prompt is the instruction block below, followed by
\texttt{Tokens: <space-separated gold tokens>} and \texttt{Tree:}. The few-shot
prompt inserts $k{=}3$ \texttt{Tokens}/\texttt{Tree} pairs, drawn from the
\emph{training} split only, between the instructions and the target sentence.
Decoding is at temperature $0$.

\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
You are an expert syntactic parser for historical Yiddish, annotated in the style
of the Penn Parsed Corpus of Historical Yiddish (PPCHY): clause labels such as
IP-MAT / IP-SUB / CP-QUE, phrase labels with function tags such as NP-SBJ /
NP-OB1 / NP-PRD / PP / ADVP-TMP, and a POS preterminal above every token.

RULES:

1. Output ONLY the raw bracketed S-expression tree, nothing else.

2. Do NOT wrap the output in markdown code fences.

3. Use EXACTLY the given tokens as the leaves, in the given order -- do not split,
merge, add, or drop tokens. Put exactly one POS preterminal above each token.
\end{quote}

Rule 3 is the one the models most often violate, and
Section~\ref{sec:results-llm} treats compliance with it as a separate measurement
from parse quality. We note that the instruction names \textsc{np-ob1} as an
example function tag, which is a label from a sibling Penn corpus rather than this
PPCHY release; this may contribute to the label-variant confusions in
Appendix~\ref{app:labels}, and a corrected prompt is an obvious follow-up.
