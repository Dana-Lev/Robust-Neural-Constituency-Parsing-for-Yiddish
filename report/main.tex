\subsection{Data}
We use the Penn Parsed Corpus of Historical Yiddish (PPCHY), converted from its
romanized form into Hebrew script with the \texttt{ppchyprep} utility of
\citet{kulick-etal-2022-part}, so that the conversion matches their published
setup. Trees are normalised under a single \textsc{top} root, split 90/5/5 with
a fixed seed, and filtered for structures the parser cannot consume.
Table~\ref{tab:data} reports the resulting statistics.

We retain \emph{all} PPCHY components rather than restricting to the two largest
20th-century texts. This is deliberate: the older material carries the greatest
orthographic variation, and therefore the heaviest subword fragmentation, which
is precisely the phenomenon under test here. We note that
\citet{halfon-boiman-2026-yiddish} describe following the two-text selection of
\citet{kulick-etal-2022-part}, but report split sizes identical to ours
(15{,}394/855/856), which are only reachable from the full corpus; we therefore
treat their results as whole-corpus numbers.

\subsection{Frontier-LLM Control Experiment}
% This answers the instructor's question: is a custom model still needed?
The research question this project inherits was posed in 2022. Before building a
dedicated parser it is therefore worth asking whether a current general-purpose
model already solves the task. We sample 30 sentences from the PPCHY
\emph{test} split (seed 1, sentences of at most 40 tokens) and prompt Gemini
\citep{geminiteam-2023-gemini} to return a PPCHY-style bracketed tree at
temperature 0, given the gold tokenization and instructed to reuse those tokens
exactly. Few-shot runs draw $k{=}3$ exemplars from the \emph{training} split
only, so no evaluated sentence is ever shown to the model.

Predictions are scored with the same conventions as the SuPar evaluation
(preterminals excluded, \textsc{top} and punctuation deleted, co-indexation
suffixes stripped, \textsc{advp}$\equiv$\textsc{prt}), which makes the Labeled
F1 directly comparable to the parser's. We additionally report three diagnostics
that a single F1 conflates: \emph{coverage}, the share of requests the API
actually returned; \emph{syntactic validity}, the share of returned answers that
parse as well-formed trees; and \emph{token fidelity}, the share that reproduce
the given tokens exactly. Reporting an API failure as a model error would
understate the model, so all behavioural rates are computed over responses
received and coverage is reported separately.

\paragraph{The LLM control.} Table~\ref{tab:llm} shows that a current frontier
model is a competent but non-conformant parser of historical Yiddish. Every
answer it returned was a well-formed tree, and 95.5\% reproduced the supplied
tokenization exactly, so the failures are not formatting failures. The
informative result is the gap between the two F1 measures: unlabeled F1 is
79.58 while labeled F1 is 54.67, a difference of 24.9 points, and 18 of the 22
scored sentences have a higher unlabeled than labeled score. The model recovers
much of the bracketing and then labels it wrongly.

The label errors are systematic rather than random. Only 12.5\% of predicted
constituents carry a label outside this treebank's 226-label inventory, and
those are dominated by \textsc{np-ob1} and \textsc{np-ob2} -- labels from the
Penn historical family, but from a different member of it than this PPCHY
release, which marks the same arguments \textsc{np-acc} and \textsc{np-dtv}. The
model has evidently absorbed Penn-style historical annotation in general and
selected the wrong variant of it, rather than failing to analyse Yiddish.

Even read charitably, this does not displace a trained parser: 54.67 labeled F1
is roughly 29 points below the frozen-encoder baseline, and even the model's
\emph{unlabeled} score does not reach the baseline's \emph{labeled} score.

\section*{Limitations}
Our evidence has several bounds. The treebank is historical, so conclusions may
not transfer to modern Yiddish; we test one encoder and one parsing formalism.
The LLM control uses 30 test sentences, and free-tier rate limits reduced
zero-shot coverage to 22 of those 30, so its confidence interval is wide and the
comparison should be read as an order-of-magnitude check rather than a precise
measurement. LLM results also depend on the prompt and on a model version that
providers change without notice; we report the exact model identifier and date
